This is about backup power for a flat in Ukraine, where Russian strikes on the energy system have brought scheduled blackouts back this autumn. In early November, according to DTEK, Kyiv Oblast was without power for 13 to 17 hours a day, some outage groups for up to 17.5. With schedules like that the question is no longer whether you need backup power, but which kind and how much.

Step by step: how it works, how to size the capacity for your own appliances, what the options are and what they cost. Prices are in hryvnias as of November 2026, the «from – to» range across shops listed on Hotline, Ukraine's price-comparison site.

How it works

Every backup system for a flat has three parts, even when they share one case:

  • The battery stores energy. Its capacity is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) — the same unit as on your electricity bill.
  • The inverter turns the battery's direct current into ordinary 230-volt alternating current for your appliances. Its power is in watts: how much it can run at the same time.
  • The charger refills the battery while the grid is on. Its current decides whether the battery is full again before the next outage.

Two numbers not to confuse: power (W) says what can run at once, capacity (kWh) says for how long. A 3 kW inverter on a 1 kWh battery will start a kettle, and the kettle will drain it in half an hour.

Most of these systems work as a UPS: while the grid is on, appliances run from it and the battery charges. When the power goes, the system switches to the battery by itself — a router or a computer usually does not even restart.

First, work out what has to run

The most expensive mistake is buying «something powerful» without the arithmetic. Start with the appliances you cannot do without during an outage and what they draw. Below are figures for popular models from their manufacturers' data.

A word on the fridge. The label gives yearly consumption: 250 kWh a year is about 0.7 kWh a day, or 30 W on average. But the compressor runs in cycles: when it is on it takes 100–200 W, and at start-up a short surge several times that. So a fridge needs inverter headroom as well as capacity: take an inverter of at least 1 kW even though the fridge «eats» 30 W on average.

How to size the capacity. Add up the power of everything that will run at once and multiply by the hours without power. Then divide by 0.85: some energy is lost in the inverter (about 90–95 % efficient by manufacturers' data sheets), and the battery should not be run down to zero.

Option 1. A portable power station

Battery, inverter and charger in one case. Put it down, plug things in, done. No electrician and no dedicated space. Current stations use LiFePO₄ (lithium iron phosphate) — a chemistry good for thousands of cycles and far less prone to catching fire than ordinary lithium-ion.

Who it suits: tenants, anyone who would rather not touch the wiring, and anyone for whom internet, light and the fridge are enough. It comes to roughly 30,000 hryvnias per kilowatt-hour.

Downsides: appliances plug into the station, not into the flat's sockets. A boiler wired straight to the mains will not run from it without rewiring. And capacity is expensive: 4 kWh in a station costs two to three times what it costs in a separate setup.

Option 2. An inverter and a separate battery

The same three parts as separate devices. It needs installing, but each kilowatt-hour is cheaper, and the system can be wired into the flat's distribution board so that the ordinary sockets and the boiler run from it.

The key choice is the battery voltage: 12, 24 or 48 volts. The higher the voltage, the lower the current for the same power, the thinner the cables and the better the efficiency. 12 V suits a small 1 kW system; 24 V suits 3 kW; 48 V suits 5 kW with a 5 kWh battery.

Compare the price per kilowatt-hour: a large 48 V setup gives 5 kWh for roughly what a 2 kWh station costs. That is why long outages, and a boiler above all, usually lead to a separate setup.

Why not lead-acid batteries

A 12 V 100 Ah AGM lead-acid battery costs 8,399 – 9,464 UAH (Ritar RA12-100) — almost half the price of a lithium one of the same rating. It is cheap only on paper. A lead-acid battery should not be discharged below half on a regular basis, so you get about 0.6 kWh out of it, not 1.2. It lasts hundreds of cycles, while LiFePO₄ is rated for 2,500–6,000 cycles by manufacturers' data sheets. With daily outages the lead-acid battery will need replacing within a year or two. And lead-acid batteries give off hydrogen while charging, so they do not belong in an unventilated living room.

Will the battery recharge in time

This is often forgotten. If the power comes back for 4–6 hours between outages, the battery has to take back everything it gave in that window. Check the charging current or charging power in the specs:

  • DELTA-class power stations recharge from a socket in about one to two hours;
  • in a separate setup the inverter sets the pace: a Victron MultiPlus-II 48/3000, for example, charges at up to 35 A, about 1.7 kW — a 5 kWh battery fills from empty in about three hours.

One more detail: an inverter draws power itself even when it supplies nothing. For a Victron MultiPlus-II that is 11–13 W by its data sheet. Over a 15-hour outage that is almost 0.2 kWh simply gone. For a small system it is a noticeable share.

Safety in a flat

  • Choose LiFePO₄. It is much more stable than ordinary lithium-ion, and it is what current power stations and 48 V rack batteries use.
  • Do not cover it or push it against a wall. Inverters and stations get warm and need air. Keep them away from radiators and out of the escape route.
  • Buy proper cables and fuses. A 1 kW system at 12 V carries close to 100 amps; a thin wire will simply melt.
  • Watch for heat. Ukraine's State Emergency Service advises using only sound chargers and cables and stopping if a device gets unusually hot.

Wiring into the distribution board — only through a changeover switch

If you want the ordinary sockets or the boiler to run from the inverter, it is connected to the flat's distribution board. One rule here is absolute: the inverter must never feed power back into the grid. During an outage, line workers may be on the cables, and voltage from your inverter can kill them.

So a changeover switch, manual or automatic (an ATS), goes between the grid, the inverter and the flat, physically preventing both sources from running together. Ukraine's retail electricity market rules, as amended by the energy regulator NEURC's resolution No. 1993 of 26 November 2024, explicitly require backup supply equipment that makes simultaneous supply from your own source and from the grid operator impossible. An electrician does this wiring, not the owner following a video.

Is there state support

We found no direct compensation for an individual flat. There are programmes for whole buildings:

  • SvitloDIM (through the Diia app) — for condominium associations (OSBB), housing co-operatives and managed buildings: 100,000 UAH for buildings up to 6 floors, 200,000 for 7–16 floors, up to 300,000 for taller ones or those with their own boiler house. The money goes on generators, inverters and batteries for the whole building. It started in January 2026 in Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast, with other regions added later.
  • Kyiv separately compensates condominium associations for part of the cost of generators, stations, inverters and batteries. Check this year's terms and deadlines with the district administration.

A lift, stairwell lighting and pumps running from such equipment make outages easier too — it is worth asking your association whether it has applied.

In short: what to choose

  1. Only internet and light for a few hours — a 0.3–0.8 kWh station, 15,000–40,000 UAH.
  2. Internet, light and the fridge through a whole outage block — a station or a small setup of 1–2 kWh, 25,000–60,000 UAH.
  3. The boiler and long outages — a 48 V setup with a 5 kWh battery, wired into the board through a changeover switch, from 85,000 UAH plus installation.

What it does to the electricity bill

A battery does not make energy — it moves it. Everything you use during an outage the system later takes from the grid, plus a little extra for charging and discharging losses and the inverter's own draw. So monthly consumption goes up slightly even though you used the same appliances.

If you have a two-zone meter, part of that charging may fall into the night zone — then every kilowatt-hour «moved» from night to day costs less.
